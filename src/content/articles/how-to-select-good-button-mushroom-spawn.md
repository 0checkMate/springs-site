Good button mushroom spawn is fresh, evenly covered in healthy white mycelium, free of off-colours and off-smells, clearly labelled, and supplied by someone who can tell you how it was made and checked. Check these things before you pay, because spawn is the starting point of your whole crop: a weak or contaminated batch costs you compost, labour and weeks of production.

## What spawn is, and why quality matters

Spawn is mushroom mycelium grown through cereal grain. You mix it into prepared compost, and the mycelium spreads from each grain to colonise the compost before the crop forms. Because it is a living culture, its condition on the day you use it directly affects how quickly and how cleanly your compost is colonised.

Two things decide that condition: how well the spawn was made, and how old and how well stored it is by the time it reaches your compost.

## What good spawn looks like

When you receive a batch, look for the following.

- **Even white growth.** Fine white mycelium visible through the grain, binding it lightly, with no large patches of bare, uncolonised grain.
- **A clean, fresh smell.** A mild, mushroom-like or earthy smell. Sour, rotten or sharp smells are a warning.
- **No colour other than white to off-white.** No green, black, orange, pink or yellow patches.
- **Grain that is damp but not wet.** No pooled liquid, slime or sticky wet patches, especially at the bottom of the container.
- **Intact, clean packaging.** Sealed, undamaged and labelled with the strain or type, the batch number and the production date.
- **Cool on arrival.** Spawn that has sat in a hot vehicle or in the sun has already lost value.

## Warning signs to reject or question

| What you see | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| Fluffy white growth that rises above the grain, later turning green | Green mould (*Trichoderma*), a serious competitor of button mushrooms. Healthy mushroom mycelium looks tighter and rope-like, while this mould looks loose and fluffy. |
| Grey, slimy, very wet grain with a sour smell, often in patches at the bottom | Bacterial contamination, commonly called wet spot or sour rot. |
| Orange, black or other coloured patches | Another mould. Do not use the batch. |
| Many grains with no mycelium | Poor or incomplete colonisation. |

Contamination can come from the grain, the equipment or the production room, which is why a supplier who controls sterilisation, inoculation and inspection matters. If you find any of these signs, do not mix the spawn into compost. Photograph the batch, keep the packaging and contact your supplier the same day.

## Freshness: how old is the spawn?

Spawn quality falls with age and with poor storage. Research on button mushroom spawn found the best yields with fresh spawn and the lowest yields after long storage, with the age of spawn identified as a major factor in its quality. As a practical rule, ask for the production date, plan your spawning so you use spawn soon after you receive it, and avoid buying more than you can use promptly.

## Questions to ask your supplier

1. Which strain or type is this, and is it suited to the compost and season I am working with?
2. When was this batch produced, and what is its batch number?
3. Is it produced in a laboratory from a maintained pure culture on sterilised grain?
4. What checks are done before the spawn is released (for example colonisation and contamination inspection)?
5. How should I transport and store it, and for how long?
6. What happens if a batch arrives contaminated or fails to grow?
7. Can you advise me on spawning rates and compost conditions for my farm?

A supplier who can answer these plainly, and in writing, is usually more reliable than one who cannot.

## How much spawn do you need?

Published guidance for button mushroom growers generally puts the spawning rate at about **0.5 to 1% of the wet weight of the compost**, depending on the strain, the compost and the grower's method. That is roughly **5 to 10 kg of spawn per tonne of compost**.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing on price alone, without asking about the production date or checks.
- Buying more spawn than you can use soon, then storing it for weeks.
- Leaving spawn in a hot vehicle or in the sun.
- Spawning compost that is still too warm or not properly prepared, which wastes good spawn.
- Ignoring early warning signs on arrival and mixing a doubtful batch into compost anyway.
